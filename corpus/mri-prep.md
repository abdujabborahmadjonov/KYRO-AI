# Preparing a Child for an MRI

An MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) machine takes detailed pictures of the inside of the body using magnets and radio waves. It does not use radiation, and nothing about the scan touches or hurts the child. The machine looks like a large tube or donut, and the table slides slowly into it.

The MRI is loud. During the scan it makes knocking, thumping, and buzzing sounds — some as loud as a lawn mower. This is normal and is simply the sound of the magnets working while the machine takes pictures. Children are given headphones or earplugs, and many centers can play music or a movie soundtrack during the scan.

Holding still is the most important job a child has during an MRI. Movement makes the pictures blurry, and blurry pictures sometimes mean parts of the scan must be repeated. Explaining this as a job or mission ("hold still like a statue so the camera gets a clear picture") helps children succeed.

A parent or caregiver can usually stay in the MRI room or just outside it for the whole scan, and the technologist can always see, hear, and talk to the child through a speaker. The child is never alone, and they can speak up at any time if they need a break.

A typical pediatric MRI takes 20 to 60 minutes. If contrast dye is needed, it is given through a small IV; placing the IV feels like a quick pinch that lasts a few seconds. Most abdominal MRIs without contrast need no needles at all.

Some children may need to avoid food or drink for a few hours before the scan, especially if sedation is planned. The care team gives exact instructions ahead of time, and following them keeps the appointment on schedule.
