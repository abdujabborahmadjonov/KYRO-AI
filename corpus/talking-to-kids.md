# Age-Appropriate Communication About Procedures

Children aged 3–7 are concrete thinkers. Use short sentences, familiar comparisons (a donut, a spaceship, a camera), and avoid medical jargon. Words like "cut," "shot," or "dye" can be misheard or frightening ("dye" sounds like "die") — say "small opening," "quick poke," and "special medicine that helps the camera see."

Preparation should happen close to the event for young children — one or two days ahead for preschoolers, up to a week for school-age children. Too much lead time gives worry room to grow; too little gives no time to process.

Always validate the fear before correcting the fact. "Lots of kids wonder about the loud sound — great question" works better than "there's nothing to be scared of." Naming the fear reduces it; dismissing it teaches the child to hide it.

Give truthful sensory previews: what the child will see, hear, feel, and smell, in order. Children who know the machine will thump loudly are startled less than children who were only told "it's easy." Predictability is the antidote to fear.

End every preparation conversation with the child's competence: what their job is, and that they can do it. Framing the child as the hero of the visit — with the care team as their crew — builds mastery instead of dread.
